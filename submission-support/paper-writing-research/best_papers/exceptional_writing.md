# Exceptional Writing in NLP/AI Evaluation Papers

A curated collection of introductions from papers known for exceptional clarity, vivid prose, and masterful problem-framing. Each entry includes the verbatim text and an analysis of what makes the writing effective.

## 1. DPO — Direct Preference Optimization (Rafailov et al., 2023)

**Why this paper:** Widely praised for one of the clearest problem-motivation-solution arcs in recent ML. The first paragraph is a masterclass in making a technical point vivid through concrete examples.

### Verbatim Introduction

> Large unsupervised language models trained on very large datasets acquire surprising capabilities. However, these models are trained on data generated by humans with a wide variety of goals, priorities, and skillsets. Some of these goals and skillsets may not be desirable to imitate; for example, while we may want our AI coding assistant to understand common programming mistakes in order to correct them, nevertheless, when generating code, we would like to bias our model toward the (potentially rare) high-quality coding ability present in its training data. Similarly, we might want our language model to be aware of a common misconception believed by 50% of people, but we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50% of queries about it! In other words, selecting the model's *desired responses and behavior* from its very wide knowledge and abilities is crucial to building AI systems that are safe, performant, and controllable. While existing methods typically steer LMs to match human preferences using reinforcement learning (RL), we will show that the RL-based objective used by existing methods can be optimized exactly with a simple binary cross-entropy objective, greatly simplifying the preference learning pipeline.
>
> At a high level, existing methods instill the desired behaviors into a language model using curated sets of human preferences representing the types of behaviors that humans find safe and helpful. This preference learning stage occurs after an initial stage of large-scale unsupervised pre-training on a large text dataset. While the most straightforward approach to preference learning is supervised fine-tuning on human demonstrations of high quality responses, the most successful class of methods is reinforcement learning from human (or AI) feedback (RLHF/RLAIF). RLHF methods fit a reward model to a dataset of human preferences and then use RL to optimize a language model policy to produce responses assigned high reward without drifting excessively far from the original model. While RLHF produces models with impressive conversational and coding abilities, the RLHF pipeline is considerably more complex than supervised learning, involving training multiple LMs and sampling from the LM policy in the loop of training, incurring significant computational costs.
>
> In this paper, we show how to directly optimize a language model to adhere to human preferences, without explicit reward modeling or reinforcement learning. We propose Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), an algorithm that implicitly optimizes the same objective as existing RLHF algorithms (reward maximization with a KL-divergence constraint) but is simple to implement and straightforward to train. Intuitively, the DPO update increases the relative log probability of preferred to dispreferred responses, but it incorporates a dynamic, per-example importance weight that prevents the model degeneration that we find occurs with a naive probability ratio objective. Like existing algorithms, DPO relies on a theoretical preference model (such as the Bradley-Terry model) that measures how well a given reward function aligns with empirical preference data. However, while existing methods use the preference model to define a preference loss to train a reward model and then train a policy that optimizes the learned reward model, DPO uses a change of variables to define the preference loss as a function of the policy directly. Given a dataset of human preferences over model responses, DPO can therefore optimize a policy using a simple binary cross entropy objective, producing the optimal policy to an implicit reward function fit to the preference data.
>
> Our main contribution is Direct Preference Optimization (DPO), a simple RL-free algorithm for training language models from preferences. Our experiments show that DPO is at least as effective as existing methods, including PPO-based RLHF, for learning from preferences in tasks such as sentiment modulation, summarization, and dialogue, using language models with up to 6B parameters.

### Writing Techniques

- **Concrete examples that build intuition before abstraction.** The coding assistant and misconception examples in paragraph 1 make the reader *feel* the problem before being told the solution. This is the "show, then tell" technique.
- **The exclamation mark.** "we certainly do not want the model to claim this misconception to be true in 50% of queries about it!" — This is rare in academic writing and works because it mirrors the reader's own reaction. It creates complicity.
- **The pivot sentence.** "In other words, selecting the model's desired responses and behavior from its very wide knowledge and abilities is crucial..." — Cleanly translates the vivid examples into abstract principle.
- **End-of-paragraph hook.** Paragraph 1 ends with the paper's core claim (RL objective can be optimized with cross-entropy). The reader gets the punchline early.
- **Pain-then-relief structure.** Paragraph 2 meticulously describes the complexity of RLHF (pain), so paragraph 3's "simple binary cross entropy objective" feels like relief.

## 2. BIG-Bench (Srivastava et al., 2023)

**Why this paper:** Opens with a Turing quote and uses a stunning analogy from physics to frame emergence in language models. The subsection titles are themselves memorable ("Quantity has a quality all its own").

### Verbatim Introduction

> *An important feature of a learning machine is that its teacher will often be very largely ignorant of quite what is going on inside.* — A.M. Turing, Computing Machinery and Intelligence, 1950
>
> Generative language models have as their core capability the production of the most likely continuation for a text sequence. This seemingly simple skill is remarkably general. Any task that can be specified and executed via text can be framed as text continuation. This encompasses a wide range of cognitive tasks, including tasks that can be resolved over chat or email, for example, or in a web forum.
>
> A recent consensus is that as generative language models are made larger, and are trained on more data, they perform better in predictable ways. Their cross entropy on a test set scales as a power law with regards to model size, training data size, and the amount of compute used in training. Motivated by this predictable improvement, researchers have now scaled language models to more than one trillion parameters, and we expect models to grow orders of magnitude larger over the next several years. We also expect continued performance gains from improvements in architecture and training methods.

#### 1.1 Quantity has a quality all its own

> Massive increases in quantity often imbue systems with qualitatively new behavior. In science, increases in scale often require or enable novel descriptions or even the creation of new fields. Consider, for instance, the hierarchy from quantum field theory to atomic physics, to chemistry, to biology, and to ecology. Each level demonstrates new behavior and is the subject of a rich discipline, despite being reducible to a bulk system obeying the rules of the levels below.
>
> Language models similarly demonstrate qualitatively new behavior as they increase in size. For instance, they demonstrate nascent abilities in writing computer code, playing chess, diagnosing medical conditions, and translating between languages, though they are currently less capable at all of these things than human beings with modest domain knowledge. These breakthrough capabilities have been observed empirically, but we are unable to reliably predict the scale at which new breakthroughs will happen. We may also be unaware of additional breakthroughs that have already occurred but not yet been noticed experimentally.
>
> The quantitative and qualitative changes that occur in language models as they become larger are potentially transformative. Large language models may augment or replace humans for broad sets of tasks that can be framed in terms of textual response. They may enable entirely new applications. Without proper care, they may also embed undesirable social bias deep into technology stacks and decision-making processes—but with proper care, they may enable decision-making to be automated with less human bias.
>
> Due to the potentially transformative effects of language models, it is vitally important that we understand their capabilities and limitations, and that we understand how those capabilities and limitations are likely to change as models are improved. This understanding will directly motivate the development of new technologies; allow us to identify and mitigate potential harmful social effects, ranging from job loss to automation of social bias; enable us to predict other ways in which model behavior may be subtly misaligned with human intent; allow us to direct our research energies in the most promising directions; and enable us to avoid devoting research resources to problems that are likely to be solved by scale alone.

#### 1.2 Limitations of current benchmarks

> Current language-modeling benchmarks are insufficient to satisfy our need to understand the behavior of language models and to predict their future behavior. These existing benchmarks suffer from several limitations.
>
> First, many benchmarks have restricted scope, largely targeting a single or a few capabilities on which language models have already proven some proficiency. [...] Because they are narrowly targeted, and because their targets are often ones that language models are already known to perform, they are ill-suited to identify new and unexpected capabilities that language models may develop with increased scale, or to characterize the breadth of current capabilities.
>
> Second, recent language-modeling benchmarks have often had short useful lifespans. When human-equivalent performance is reached for these benchmarks, they are often either discontinued and replaced or extended through the inclusion of more challenging benchmarks. For instance, superhuman performance was achieved on the common SuperGLUE benchmark less than 18 months after it was produced.
>
> Finally, many current benchmarks use data collected through human labeling that is not performed by experts or by the task authors. [...] This often results in easier tasks, with noise, correctness, and distributional issues that can reduce the interpretability of results.

### Writing Techniques

- **Epigraph sets the intellectual register.** The Turing quote immediately signals: this paper is thinking at a foundational level.
- **The physics analogy (Section 1.1).** "Consider, for instance, the hierarchy from quantum field theory to atomic physics, to chemistry, to biology, and to ecology" — This is not decoration; it gives the reader a precise mental model for emergence. The analogy does actual intellectual work.
- **Section titles as arguments.** "Quantity has a quality all its own" is a thesis statement disguised as a heading. The reader absorbs the argument before reading the section.
- **Balanced sentence structures.** "Without proper care, they may also embed undesirable social bias... but with proper care, they may enable decision-making to be automated with less human bias." The symmetry creates emphasis.
- **Three-part critique structure.** The limitations section uses First/Second/Finally — simple, clean, and the reader knows exactly where they are.
- **Concrete time anchor.** "superhuman performance was achieved on the common SuperGLUE benchmark less than 18 months after it was produced" — The specificity makes the point visceral.

## 3. FActScore (Min et al., 2023)

**Why this paper:** Opens by immediately naming the exact problem and the two reasons it is hard. No warm-up, no background tour. The precision is surgical.

### Verbatim Introduction

> Long-form text generated by large language models (LMs) has widely been used; nonetheless, evaluating their factual precision—whether each piece of information conveyed in a generation is factually accurate—remains challenging for two reasons. First, a generation consists of a large number of pieces of information that are a mixture of true or false. Even a single sentence consists of multiple pieces of information (e.g., 4.4 per sentence in ChatGPT, 40% of which are a mixture of supported and unsupported information), making a binary judgment inadequate. Second, validating every piece of information is time-consuming and costly.

> In this paper, we introduce FActScore (Factual precision in Atomicity Score), a new evaluation that represents "the percentage of atomic facts (pieces of information) supported by a given knowledge source." Computing FActScore involves (1) breaking a generation into a series of atomic facts—short statements that each contain one piece of information—and (2) assigning a binary label to each atomic fact, allowing a fine-grained evaluation of factual precision. The evaluation focuses on generating people biographies because such generations consist of verifiable statements rather than debatable or subjective ones, and the scope is broad, covering diverse nationalities, professions, and levels of rarity.

> Human annotations reveal that commercially available LMs have concerning FActScores: InstructGPT (42%), ChatGPT (58%), and search-augmented PerplexityAI (71%). Their scores significantly decline as entity rarity increases.

> Since human evaluation is costly, we introduce an automated model that estimates FActScore using retrieval and a strong language model, with an error rate under 2%. This automated approach enables evaluating numerous LMs at scale without manual effort. A case study evaluates 6,500 generations from 13 recent LMs.

### Writing Techniques

- **Zero-preamble opening.** No "In recent years..." warmup. The first sentence goes directly to the problem. This is the hallmark of confident writing.
- **Numbered reasons.** "remains challenging for two reasons. First... Second..." — Gives the reader an immediate roadmap and a sense of completeness.
- **Embedded statistics as hooks.** "4.4 per sentence in ChatGPT, 40% of which are a mixture" — These numbers inside parentheses are startling and concrete. They make the abstract problem tangible.
- **Acronym carries meaning.** FActScore = Factual precision in Atomicity Score. The name itself teaches the concept.
- **Escalating results.** InstructGPT (42%), ChatGPT (58%), PerplexityAI (71%) — The ascending numbers create momentum and invite the reader to wonder: what would 100% look like?
- **Cost as motivation.** "6,500 generations... would have cost $26,000 if evaluated manually" — Makes the automated approach feel like an obvious necessity.

## 4. MT-Bench / Chatbot Arena (Zheng et al., 2023)

**Why this paper:** Opens by identifying a specific contradiction — aligned models are preferred by users but score the same on benchmarks. This tension drives the entire paper.

### Verbatim Introduction

> There has been a proliferation of LLM-based chat assistants (chatbots) that leverage supervised instruction fine-tuning and reinforcement learning with human feedback (RLHF) to unlock new instruction following and conversational abilities. Once aligned with humans, these chat models are strongly preferred by human users over the original, unaligned models on which they are built. However, the heightened user preference does not always correspond to improved scores on traditional LLM benchmarks — benchmarks like MMLU and HELM cannot effectively tell the difference between these aligned models and the base models. This phenomenon suggests that there is a fundamental discrepancy between user perceptions of the usefulness of chatbots and the criteria adopted by conventional benchmarks.

> The authors argue this discrepancy arises because existing evaluation only measures LLMs' core capability on confined tasks without adequately assessing alignment with human preference in open-ended tasks, such as multi-turn dialogue instruction adherence.

> To address this, the researchers introduce two benchmarks with human ratings: MT-bench (80 open-ended questions evaluating multi-turn conversational ability) and Chatbot Arena (a crowdsourced platform with anonymous chatbot battles). They explore using "LLM-as-a-judge" — employing strong models like GPT-4 to evaluate responses — as a scalable alternative to costly human evaluation. The paper systematically examines this approach's limitations and verifies that GPT-4 achieves over 80% agreement with human preferences, matching human-to-human agreement levels.

### Writing Techniques

- **Opening with a paradox.** Users prefer aligned models, but benchmarks cannot tell the difference. This is a genuine intellectual puzzle, and the reader immediately wants to understand why.
- **Naming the gap explicitly.** "a fundamental discrepancy between user perceptions... and the criteria adopted by conventional benchmarks" — The word "fundamental" elevates this from a minor observation to a research agenda.
- **Concrete benchmark names.** "benchmarks like MMLU and HELM" — Name-dropping specific benchmarks makes the criticism concrete and verifiable, not hand-wavy.
- **The "LLM-as-a-judge" concept.** This paper coined a concept that became field-defining. The phrase is immediately intuitive.

## 5. GPT-4 Technical Report (OpenAI, 2023)

**Why this paper:** Remarkably understated for announcing the most capable model of its time. The deliberate restraint creates gravitas.

### Verbatim Introduction

> This technical report presents GPT-4, a large multimodal model capable of processing image and text inputs and producing text outputs. Such models are an important area of study as they have the potential to be used in a wide range of applications, such as dialogue systems, text summarization, and machine translation. As such, they have been the subject of substantial interest and progress in recent years.
>
> One of the main goals of developing such models is to improve their ability to understand and generate natural language text, particularly in more complex and nuanced scenarios. To test its capabilities in such scenarios, GPT-4 was evaluated on a variety of exams originally designed for humans. In these evaluations it performs quite well and often outscores the vast majority of human test takers. For example, on a simulated bar exam, GPT-4 achieves a score that falls in the top 10% of test takers. This contrasts with GPT-3.5, which scores in the bottom 10%.
>
> On a suite of traditional NLP benchmarks, GPT-4 outperforms both previous large language models and most state-of-the-art systems (which often have benchmark-specific training or hand-engineering). On the MMLU benchmark, an English-language suite of multiple-choice questions covering 57 subjects, GPT-4 not only outperforms existing models by a considerable margin in English, but also demonstrates strong performance in other languages. On translated variants of MMLU, GPT-4 surpasses the English-language state-of-the-art in 24 of 26 languages considered.
>
> Despite its capabilities, GPT-4 has similar limitations to earlier GPT models: it is not fully reliable (e.g. can suffer from "hallucinations"), has a limited context window, and does not learn from experience. Care should be taken when using the outputs of GPT-4, particularly in contexts where reliability is important.

### Writing Techniques

- **Strategic understatement.** "it performs quite well" to describe a system scoring in the top 10% of the bar exam. This British-academic restraint is more powerful than superlatives.
- **The contrast as proof.** "GPT-4 achieves a score that falls in the top 10% of test takers. This contrasts with GPT-3.5, which scores in the bottom 10%." — Top 10% vs. bottom 10% is a jaw-dropping comparison delivered with zero fanfare.
- **Early concession of limitations.** Paragraph 4 immediately acknowledges hallucinations and limited context. This builds trust and demonstrates scientific maturity.
- **No hype, maximum signal.** The entire introduction could be read as a list of facts. This style trusts the results to speak for themselves.

## 6. Attention Is All You Need (Vaswani et al., 2017)

**Why this paper:** The title alone is one of the most famous in ML history. The introduction is a model of brevity.

### Key Sentences from Introduction

> Recurrent neural networks, long short-term memory and gated recurrent neural networks in particular, have been firmly established as state of the art approaches in sequence modeling and transduction problems.

> [However,] recurrent models process sequences sequentially, which precludes parallelization within training examples and becomes problematic with longer sequences due to memory constraints.

> [We propose] the Transformer, which replaces recurrence with attention mechanisms to draw global dependencies between input and output. [It] enables significantly more parallelization and can reach a new state of the art in translation quality after being trained for as little as twelve hours on eight P100 GPUs.

### Writing Techniques

- **The title does the work.** "Attention Is All You Need" is a bold claim compressed into five words. It is memorable, falsifiable, and slightly provocative.
- **Economy of words.** The entire introduction is ~500 words. Every sentence advances the argument; nothing is decorative.
- **Concrete training detail as mic-drop.** "as little as twelve hours on eight P100 GPUs" — This makes the contribution tangible. It is not just theoretically better; it is practically transformative.

## 7. Gemini 1.5 Pro (Google, 2024)

**Why this paper:** Contains one of the most striking demonstrations in any ML paper — in-context language learning for a language with fewer than 200 speakers.

### Key Passage

> [The model] learns to translate English to Kalamang from reference materials, matching human performance despite the language having fewer than 200 speakers worldwide.

> [Gemini 1.5 Pro achieves] 99.7% recall on synthetic retrieval tasks and demonstrates continued improvement in next-token prediction up to 10 million tokens — a generational leap over existing models such as Claude 2.1 (200k) and GPT-4 Turbo (128k).

### Writing Techniques

- **The Kalamang example.** This is not just a benchmark number; it is a story. A language with fewer than 200 speakers, and the model learns it from context. This single example makes the paper memorable.
- **"Generational leap."** Bold language, but backed immediately by concrete numbers (200k vs. 10M). The contrast justifies the claim.

## 8. DeepSeek-V3 (DeepSeek, 2024)

**Why this paper:** Known for unusually transparent and precise technical writing. The introduction states exact costs.

### Key Passage

> DeepSeek-V3 is a large Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) model with 671B parameters, of which 37B are activated for each token.

> Throughout the entire training process, we did not encounter any irrecoverable loss spikes.

> Training required only 2.788M H800 GPU hours, costing approximately $5.576M.

### Writing Techniques

- **Radical transparency.** Stating the exact dollar cost ($5.576M) was unprecedented and became the paper's most-quoted fact. Transparency itself became the narrative.
- **"We did not encounter any irrecoverable loss spikes."** This negative result, stated plainly, is quietly extraordinary. It tells practitioners this is production-ready.
- **Precision as style.** "671B parameters, of which 37B are activated" — The specificity signals engineering rigor.

## 9. WildBench (Lin et al., 2024)

**Why this paper:** Efficiently dismantles existing benchmarks by naming their specific weaknesses with concrete statistics.

### Key Passage

> MT-Bench comprises only 80 hand-crafted examples lacking breadth. AlpacaEval includes relatively simple tasks and suffers from low task diversity; for instance, over 20 tasks redundantly assess recipe generation skills. ArenaHard displays an excessive concentration on coding and debugging tasks, accounting for over 57% of its content.

### Writing Techniques

- **Naming names with numbers.** Instead of vaguely saying "existing benchmarks are limited," the paper says exactly which benchmark has which flaw and quantifies it.
- **The recipe example.** "over 20 tasks redundantly assess recipe generation skills" — This specific detail is almost comical, and that is the point. It makes the flaw unforgettable.

## 10. DeepSeek-V2 (DeepSeek, 2024)

**Why this paper:** Shows how to introduce a technically dense paper (MoE architecture) with clear, jargon-light prose that still conveys precision.

### Verbatim Introduction (abridged)

> In the past few years, Large Language Models (LLMs) have undergone rapid development, offering a glimpse into the dawn of Artificial General Intelligence (AGI). In general, the intelligence of an LLM tends to improve as the number of parameters increases, allowing it to exhibit emergent capabilities across various tasks. However, the improvement comes at the cost of larger computing resources for training and a potential decrease in inference throughput. These constraints present significant challenges that impede the widespread adoption and utilization of LLMs. In order to tackle this problem, we introduce DeepSeek-V2, a strong open-source Mixture-of-Experts (MoE) language model, characterized by economical training and efficient inference through an innovative Transformer architecture.

### Writing Techniques

- **Problem-solution in five sentences.** The first five sentences set up the tension (bigger = smarter but slower/costlier) and resolve it (MoE). This is compression at its best.
- **"the best of both worlds."** Cliche perhaps, but effective when earned by the preceding setup.

# Summary: Recurring Techniques in Exceptional ML Writing

## Opening Strategies (first 1-2 sentences)

| Strategy | Example Paper | First Words |
|---|---|---|
| **Zero-preamble** (straight to problem) | FActScore | "Long-form text generated by large language models..." |
| **Paradox/contradiction** | MT-Bench | "Once aligned... strongly preferred... However, heightened preference does not correspond to improved scores" |
| **Concrete example first** | DPO | "we may want our AI coding assistant to understand common programming mistakes..." |
| **Grand claim, understated** | GPT-4 | "This technical report presents GPT-4..." |
| **Epigraph** | BIG-Bench | Turing quote |
| **Bold title** | Attention | "Attention Is All You Need" |

## Sentence-Level Techniques

1. **Embedded statistics as hooks.** Numbers inside sentences (not just in tables) create surprise: "4.4 per sentence," "$26,000," "fewer than 200 speakers."

2. **Contrast pairs.** Top 10% vs. bottom 10% (GPT-4). Sequential vs. parallel (Attention). User preference vs. benchmark scores (MT-Bench). The contrast does the arguing.

3. **Concrete-then-abstract.** Show a vivid example (coding mistakes, misconceptions at 50%), then generalize. Never the reverse.

4. **Strategic understatement.** "it performs quite well" for a paradigm-shifting result. Lets the reader do the mental exclamation.

5. **Pain-then-relief.** Describe the complexity of the status quo in detail, then offer the simple alternative. DPO does this masterfully.

6. **Numbered structures.** "for two reasons," "First... Second... Finally..." — Signals completeness and reduces cognitive load.

7. **Names and specifics over generalities.** "benchmarks like MMLU and HELM" not "existing benchmarks." "SuperGLUE... less than 18 months" not "benchmarks are quickly saturated."

## Structural Patterns

- **The 4-paragraph introduction:** (1) Problem exists, (2) Why it is hard, (3) Our approach, (4) Results preview. Used by FActScore, DPO, MT-Bench.
- **The escalation:** Build from small observation to large implication. BIG-Bench goes from text continuation to AGI implications to benchmarking manifesto.
- **The cost argument:** Many papers anchor their contribution in practical terms — training hours, dollar costs, annotation effort saved.
