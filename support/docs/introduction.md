# Introduction draft: SciFig-Evaluation

---

Scientific figures—line plots, bar charts, pie charts, and related graphics—are central to how researchers communicate evidence. They condense measurements, comparisons, and uncertainty into a form that readers encounter in every discipline. At the same time, multimodal large language models are increasingly used to assist literature review, accessibility, education, and scientific search, often by consuming **figure pixels** directly. That shift raises a basic but urgent question: **when a model describes or interprets a scientific figure, is it genuinely reading the visualization, or is it producing plausible language from captions, parametric priors, and textual shortcuts?** If we evaluate multimodal systems only with easy scores or closed-ended formats, we risk rewarding **fluency without faithfulness** and deploying tools that **look competent** while failing under realistic conditions: multilingual axis labels, noisy scans, misleading captions, missing information, or adversarial framing.

Recent benchmarks and analyses have made it clear that **chart and figure understanding** is far from solved. Work on **real-world charts** from scientific and general sources shows large gaps between strong models and humans, with **reasoning-style** questions often much harder than **surface reading**. Complementary lines of research document **hallucination** and **brittleness** under **unanswerable** questions, **perturbations**, or **inconsistent prompts**, and show that **multimodal** scores can overstate “visual understanding” when models can answer from **language-side recall** alone on fixed visualization question-answering tasks. Other studies diagnose **bottlenecks** in controlled visualization settings—for example, evidence that fine-grained numeric structure may be **present in visual representations** yet **lost or misused** when models produce answers—while work on **misleading** graphics and visualization literacy highlights **trust** failures that are not captured by average accuracy. **Multilingual** chart evaluation is beginning to appear, often via **synthetic or pipeline-generated** multilingual chart question answering, which addresses language coverage but does not replace the need to evaluate **open-ended** behavior on **authentic scientific figures** with **human-grounded** quality judgments.

Despite this progress, current evaluation remains **fragmented** across tasks and metrics. Chart question answering, chart captioning diagnostics, visualization literacy tests, robustness suites, and hallucination taxonomies each illuminate part of the problem, but they rarely **jointly** characterize what we care about for **scientific figures in the wild**: **multilingual**, **reference-grounded**, **open-ended description** under **adversarial and contextual stress**, with **behavioral** measures that go beyond a single accuracy number. In particular, standard metrics such as accuracy or a single quality score do not cleanly separate **honesty** (whether a model admits unreadable or unanswerable content) from **robustness** (whether it resists wrong captions, page context, or misleading prompts)—two properties that can **trade off** and that matter for any system that is meant to support scientific reading rather than entertain.

**We present SciFig-Evaluation**, a benchmark and evaluation protocol centered on **multilingual scientific figure description**. Our figure corpus comprises **1,005** figures drawn from real academic papers, spanning **four languages** (English, Chinese, Bulgarian, and German), with **1,411** human reference annotations produced by **eight** annotators, focusing on common scientific plot types (line plots, bar charts, pie charts). We evaluate **open-ended** model outputs using an **MQM-inspired** multidimensional quality framework with **multiple judge models** (**four** judges from **two** families) applied to generations from **eleven** generator models across **five** families. This design targets the gap between **fluent description** and **faithful, visually grounded scientific communication** in multilingual settings.

Beyond static description quality, we introduce a **systematic adversarial probing framework** with five complementary strategies: **(A1)** hallucination probing over absent, incorrect, irrelevant, or unanswerable content; **(A2)** caption and in-paper **context bias** to test reliance on text shortcuts versus genuine visual reading; **(A3)** **visual degradation** and text manipulation to measure robustness; **(A4)** **prompt-reverse** inconsistency probes to measure stability under contradictory framing; and **(A5)** **misleading-chart** detection with explicit attention to **false alarms** on correct figures. Together with a **capability benchmark suite** that separates **descriptive reading**, **reasoning**, and **in-paper context** understanding, these probes map **where** competence breaks down—not only **how high** the score is.

Our methodological contribution is a pair of **metrics—admittance and resistance**—that capture behaviors **orthogonal** to standard MQM or accuracy. **Admittance** measures whether models **acknowledge limitations** when information is missing, degraded, or beyond what the figure supports (for example, refusing to invent axis labels under blur or refusing to fabricate statistics when unanswerable). **Resistance** measures whether models **maintain faithful descriptions** under **misleading** conditions such as incorrect captions, distracting page text, image transforms, or adversarial prompts. We argue that both are necessary: a model can be **stubbornly confident** yet **fragile** to context, or **cautious** yet **easily misled**; existing metrics do not disentangle these failure modes. We operationalize scoring through a mix of **rule-based checks**, **LLM judges**, and **MQM deltas** under perturbation, as detailed in the measurement plan.

Finally, we report a **cross-model architecture analysis** across **seven** models from **five** families, asking whether **family** or **parameter count** better predicts **adversarial robustness**, **admittance**, and **resistance**—an analysis aimed at practitioners who must choose models under realistic scientific reading constraints.

**Contributions.** In summary, we contribute: **(1)** the **SciFig** multilingual scientific figure description benchmark with human references and multi-judge MQM-style evaluation; **(2)** a **unified adversarial probing framework** (A1–A5) for scientific figures; **(3)** a **capability suite** spanning reading, reasoning, and in-paper context; **(4)** **admittance** and **resistance** metrics for honesty and robustness beyond accuracy; and **(5)** **cross-family empirical analysis** relating architecture and scale to these behaviors. We situate SciFig-Evaluation relative to prior chart QA, captioning, literacy, robustness, and multilingual chart work, and we frame a set of **testable hypotheses**—including caption bias, parametric recall under weak vision, counting–reasoning gaps, and architecture-dependent honesty—about **when** multimodal models **see** versus **recall**, and **when** they **admit** versus **fabricate**. Together, these contributions aim to make **scientific figure understanding** evaluable in the same **multilingual, generative, and trust-sensitive** regime in which models are now deployed.

## Optional closing paragraph (limitations / ethics, tighten as needed)

Scientific figures inherit **copyright and context** from their sources; our benchmark construction follows licensing and citation norms appropriate for research use. Multilingual evaluation introduces **translation and rendering** confounds in some chart-centric pipelines; SciFig emphasizes **naturally occurring** paper figures and **human-written** references in each target language to reduce purely synthetic artifacts, though **residual** cultural and domain bias remains an open limitation we discuss in the full paper.
