\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

GPT-5.2 produces the highest-quality scientific figure descriptions in our evaluation, scoring 91.6 on a checklist-based MQM metric validated against human judgment.
It also fabricates answers 94\% of the time when asked about chart elements it cannot see, with nothing in its output to distinguish the fabrication from genuine analysis.
This is not a failure of perception or reasoning.
The model sees well and thinks competently.
It is a failure of \emph{behaviour}, the dimension that determines what a model does when it encounters uncertainty or deception, and the dimension that no existing benchmark measures.

Vision-language models are entering scientific workflows at scale, powering literature review assistants, accessibility narration tools, and automated figure analysis pipelines.
Current benchmarks evaluate these models on perception (can the model extract information from a figure?) and reasoning (can it answer targeted questions about what it sees?).
FigureQA~\citep{kahou2018figureqa} and PlotQA~\citep{methani2020plotqa} test binary and numerical reasoning over synthetic charts.
ChartQA~\citep{masry2022chartqa} advances to human-authored questions on real-world figures, and ChartBench~\citep{xu2024chartbench} broadens chart type coverage.
These benchmarks share a common design assumption: that the model receives a clean image and a well-formed question, and that the answer is knowable from the visual evidence provided.
Scientific deployment violates all three assumptions.
Figures arrive degraded, captions contain errors, and some questions have no answer in the image.
A model that scores perfectly on ChartQA can still confidently fabricate information about chart elements that do not exist, because no existing benchmark tests what happens when the ground shifts.

We present \textsc{SciFig-Eval}, a diagnostic benchmark that evaluates vision-language models across three dimensions of increasing demand.
\emph{Perception} measures whether a model can see and describe a scientific figure accurately, using checklist-based MQM scoring across 250 figures and 6 image transforms.
\emph{Reasoning} measures whether a model can think about what it sees, through targeted capability questions spanning counting, computation, comparison, and pattern analysis.
\emph{Behaviour} measures how a model acts under uncertainty and deception, through the \emph{A-R-I framework} that decomposes behavioural reliability into three independently measurable dimensions.
Admittance tests whether the model acknowledges what it cannot see.
Resistance tests whether it pushes back on false premises and misleading captions.
Inductance tests whether it reasons from partial information rather than fabricating.
The adversarial probes that drive the behavioural evaluation are grounded in cognitive science: presupposition embedding~\citep{loftus1975leading} tests whether definite articles trick models into accepting non-existent chart elements, anchoring bias~\citep{tversky1974judgment} plants plausible but incorrect values, and sycophantic agreement~\citep{sharma2024sycophancy} measures whether models echo false captions over their own visual evidence.
We evaluate 8 models across more than 11,000 evaluation instances (Figure~\ref{fig:overview}).

Models that are nearly indistinguishable on perception and reasoning diverge sharply on behaviour.
GPT-5.2 and Gemini 3.1 Pro score within 1.4 MQM points of each other on description quality, yet their admittance rates differ by 84 percentage points.
Gemini acknowledges visual uncertainty in 90\% of cases. No other model exceeds 22\%.
The divergence extends beyond admittance. Phi-4 follows poisoned captions over its own visual evidence 95\% of the time ($R = 0.05$), and presupposition embedding catches models that successfully resist explicit false values but fail against implicit assumptions about non-existent elements.
A benchmark reporting only perception and reasoning scores would rank GPT-5.2 and Gemini as near-equivalent, missing the behavioural gap that separates a confident fabricator from the only model in our evaluation that admits what it cannot see.

Our contributions are as follows:
\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textsc{SciFig-Eval}, a diagnostic benchmark that evaluates VLMs across perception, reasoning, and behaviour on scientific figures, pairing standard quality assessment with psychology-informed adversarial probes (\S\ref{sec:framework}).
    \item The A-R-I behavioural framework, which decomposes model behaviour under challenge into Admittance, Resistance, and Inductance, providing independently measurable dimensions that any evaluation can adopt (\S\ref{sec:ari}).
    \item An empirical demonstration, across 8 models and 11,000+ instances, that perception and behaviour are distinct dimensions of VLM competence, with model rankings diverging at precisely the points that matter for scientific deployment (\S\ref{sec:results}).
    \item A full benchmark release including the annotated dataset, adversarial probes, evaluation code, and an interactive review dashboard.
\end{enumerate}
