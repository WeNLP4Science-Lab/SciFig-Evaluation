\section{Benchmark and Evaluation Framework}
\label{sec:framework}

\textsc{SciFig-Eval} evaluates the perception and understanding of scientific figures by VLMs along three dimensions: \emph{perception}, whether a model accurately describes a figure based on what it perceives (sees); \emph{reasoning}, whether it answers targeted analytical questions about the visual evidence; and \emph{behaviour}, how it acts when evidence is degraded, incomplete, or contradicted by misleading context.

\subsection{Standard Evaluation}
\label{sec:mqm}

\paragraph{Perception.}
Models produce open-ended descriptions of each figure. We score them with a checklist-based adaptation of Multidimensional Quality Metrics (MQM) using chart-type-specific checklists. A GPT-4o judge evaluates coverage and correctness against expert groundtruth descriptions, and a rule engine maps errors to Accuracy, Completeness, and Clarity penalties (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-mqm-pipeline}). The final score is:

\begin{tcolorbox}[colback=gray!5, colframe=gray!50, boxrule=0.4pt, arc=2pt, left=4pt, right=4pt, top=3pt, bottom=3pt]
\small
$\text{MQM} = \max\!\bigl(0,\; 100 - P \times 100 \mathbin{/} (N \times 5)\bigr)$

\smallskip
\noindent where $P$ is the total weighted penalty and $N$ is the number of checklist items. A score of 100 indicates an error-free description.
\end{tcolorbox}

\noindent Three human annotators independently scored 120 (figure, model) pairs across 30 figures and 4 models; inter-annotator reliability was Krippendorff's $\alpha = 0.91$ (interval scale), and model-level ranking agreement with the automated judge was $\rho = 0.80$ ($n = 4$ models, $p < 0.05$). Details appear in Appendices~\ref{sec:appendix-human-validation}, \ref{sec:mqm-details}, and~\ref{sec:appendix-mqm-pipeline}.

\paragraph{Reasoning.}
Targeted reasoning is measured with 1,000 capability questions (four per figure) covering counting, computation, comparison, and pattern analysis (Table~\ref{tab:capability-categories}). Questions are generated by GPT-4o from the figure image and expert annotation, validated by Mistral Large~3, and filtered to one per category. Each answer was independently reviewed by human annotators against the source figure (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-capability-pipeline}).



\subsection{Behavioural Probes}
\label{sec:caption-bias}
\label{sec:resistance}

Behavioural evaluation uses five probe dimensions. Visual transforms test robustness to low contrast, Gaussian noise, and rotation. A page-context condition (in-paper) embeds the figure in its original PDF page to test whether models can identify and describe a target figure in context. An in-paper-blur variant blurs the figure itself within the page, measuring how much of the in-paper performance relies on surrounding page text rather than the figure (Appendix~\ref{sec:appendix-transform-pipeline}; Figure~\ref{fig:appendix-degradation-gallery}). Caption-bias probes pair figures with modified captions containing 2--3 plausible but false claims. Resistance probes test models with non-existent chart elements (\emph{inexist}), contradictory numerical premises (\emph{contra}), and unanswerable questions (\emph{unanswerable}), for example:

\begin{quote}\small
\textbf{Inexist:} ``The error bars in the third group appear wider than in the first. Does this indicate higher variance?'' (presupposition: error bars do not exist)\\
\textbf{Contra:} ``Given that Method A achieves approximately 72\% accuracy, how does this compare to Method B?'' (anchoring: actual value is 58\%)\\
\textbf{Unanswerable:} ``What is the projected compound annual growth rate for the next five years?'' (requires data not in the chart)
\end{quote}

Selective-blur probes obscure individual chart elements: admittance targets are unrecoverable once blurred, while inductance targets remain inferable from surrounding visual context. Blur targets are identified via OCR, ranked by a selector model (GPT-4o), and confirmed by human reviewers (Appendices~\ref{sec:appendix-blur-pipeline}, \ref{sec:probe-taxonomy}, and~\ref{sec:appendix-eval-pipelines}).





\subsection{The A-R-I Behavioural Framework}
\label{sec:ari}

The behavioural probes are unified by the Admittance--Resistance--Inductance (A-R-I) framework, borrowing loosely from circuit analysis to name how models handle, reject, or reconstruct visual information.

\paragraph{Admittance} measures epistemic honesty under visual uncertainty. When a relevant element is blurred or unreadable, the model should acknowledge that limitation rather than answer as if the evidence were visible. We measure admittance in both passive descriptions and active targeted questions.

\paragraph{Resistance} measures robustness to misleading context. A resistant model rejects false premises, non-existent visual elements, unanswerable requests, and modified captions rather than incorporating them into its answer.

\paragraph{Inductance} measures bounded inference from partial evidence. Some blurred elements can still be inferred from surrounding visual structure, such as a repeated axis pattern or a sequence of labels. Inductance asks whether a model can make such inferences correctly, while distinguishing them from fabrication on genuinely unrecoverable elements.

These dimensions are not exhaustive, but each targets a distinct deployment risk: admittance failures produce silent fabrication, resistance failures allow context manipulation, and inductance failures reflect an inability to reason from partial evidence when sufficient context remains. Our findings show that these dimensions are empirically separable. Models that resist false premises and reason correctly from partial context can still fail to acknowledge missing evidence, and models that admit uncertainty readily may nonetheless fabricate under targeted questioning.
