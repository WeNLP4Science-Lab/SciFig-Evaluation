\section{Practical Implications}\label{sec:implications}
%% ===================================================================

The findings of this thesis have direct consequences for several practitioner communities.

\paragraph{Agent pipeline design.}
In multi-agent systems and agentic workflows where one model's output feeds another's input, a confident but incorrect intermediate result can cascade into downstream failures.
The Admittance axis provides a direct signal for this risk: models with low admittance will silently fabricate rather than flag uncertainty, making them unsuitable for pipeline stages where precision is critical.
Engineers designing such systems should evaluate candidate models on admittance before deployment, selecting models that surface uncertainty rather than suppress it.

\paragraph{Model selection and routing.}
The A-R-I profiles demonstrate that models have distinct behavioural strengths.
No model dominates all three axes equally, though some approach this ideal.
For systems that route queries to different models based on task type, the behavioural profiles provide actionable selection criteria: admittance for uncertainty-critical tasks, resistance for adversarial-facing contexts, and inductance for reasoning-intensive queries.
The rank flow analysis (Figure~\ref{fig:rank-flow}) further shows that a model's description quality ranking does not predict its comprehension or behavioural ranking, reinforcing that model selection must be task-aware.

\paragraph{Accessibility tool development.}
The numerical precision bottleneck (mean 2.7 incorrect values per figure) means that VLM-generated descriptions of scientific figures cannot yet be trusted without verification for tasks requiring exact values.
Accessibility tools should present descriptions with appropriate confidence caveats, particularly for chart types that receive lower-quality output (e.g., pie charts).
The cross-lingual performance gaps indicate that tools serving multilingual communities must account for substantial quality variation across languages, especially for smaller models.

\paragraph{Benchmark design.}
Single-score benchmarks systematically miss behavioural dimensions.
Our rank flow analysis demonstrates that model rankings shuffle across evaluation axes, meaning a leaderboard based on description quality alone misrepresents model suitability for deployment.
Future benchmarks should incorporate behavioural probes alongside accuracy metrics, and cross-lingual claims require controlled parallel figure designs to avoid dataset composition confounds.

\section{Limitations}\label{sec:limitations}
%% ===================================================================

\paragraph{Human evaluation scope.}
Our human evaluation covers four models on 30 English figures with three annotators (159 annotations, 39 double-annotated).
While inter-annotator agreement is strong (ICC\,=\,.91), broader coverage across all 13 models and all four languages would strengthen the judge validation.

\paragraph{Cross-lingual controlled sample.}
The controlled cross-lingual comparison uses only 13 parallel figures.
Although this is sufficient to demonstrate the dataset composition confound, a larger parallel set would increase statistical power and enable per-chart-type cross-lingual analysis.

\paragraph{Inductance sample size.}
Only 9 inferable elements exist across the 45-figure adversarial set.
This limits the granularity of inductance analysis and prevents per-category breakdown of inference types.

\paragraph{Evaluation framework artefacts.}
Three systematic artefacts affect scoring.
First, colour synonym false positives penalise semantically equivalent descriptions (${\sim}5\%$ on English), and multiple mitigation attempts (compound rules, few-shot examples, self-reflection) failed.
Second, dense atoms inflate pie chart penalties by ${\sim}11$ points through error multiplication.
Third, the verbosity penalty in MQM-based evaluation may disadvantage models that produce the thorough descriptions accessibility users need.

\paragraph{Judge-dependent absolute scores.}
GPT-4o assigns 78\% Major severity vs Mistral's 47\% on the same errors.
While model rankings are robust ($\rho_{\text{sys}}\geq.95$), absolute scores depend heavily on judge selection.
Per-figure scores correlate only moderately with humans ($\rho{\approx}0.6$), meaning individual figure assessments carry substantial uncertainty.

\paragraph{Model coverage.}
Claude Opus 4.6 and Phi-4 Multimodal were evaluated on fewer figures (100 and 45 respectively) than the other 11 models (120 each).
All models were evaluated at a single temperature (0) and with a fixed prompt, limiting generalisability to other decoding configurations.

\section{Ethical Considerations}\label{sec:ethics}
%% ===================================================================

\paragraph{Data provenance.}
All 1\,005 figures in the SciFig corpus are drawn from publicly available scientific papers on arXiv and open-access repositories.
No personally identifiable information appears in the dataset.
Ground-truth annotations were produced by trained annotators following documented guidelines, with informed consent for the annotation task.

\paragraph{Model bias and accessibility.}
Our findings reveal that VLM performance varies substantially across languages, with Bulgarian and Chinese figures receiving lower-quality descriptions than English and German.
This disparity has direct implications for equitable access to scientific content: researchers working in under-resourced languages receive less reliable automated descriptions.
We report these disparities transparently to inform deployment decisions.

\paragraph{LLM judge bias.}
The dual-judge evaluation inherits biases from GPT-4o and Mistral Large 3, including the severity split documented in Chapter~\ref{ch:discussion}.
We mitigate this through judge averaging and human validation, but acknowledge that the evaluation framework reflects the perspectives of two specific commercial models.

\paragraph{Responsible deployment.}
Our results demonstrate that even the best-performing model (GPT-5.2) produces ${\sim}2.7$ incorrect numerical values per figure.
We caution against deploying VLM-generated descriptions as authoritative without human verification, particularly in contexts where numerical precision is critical (e.g., clinical or regulatory figures).

\section{Future Work}\label{sec:future-work}
%% ===================================================================

\paragraph{Finer-grained atom checklists.}
Splitting dense atoms into single-fact units (one verifiable claim per atom) would eliminate the granularity artefact that inflates pie chart penalties.
This requires substantial re-annotation effort but would produce a fairer scoring baseline.

\paragraph{Broader human evaluation.}
Extending human annotation to all 13 models across all four languages would enable per-language judge validation and reveal whether the judge harshness bias varies across scripts and languages.

\paragraph{Larger parallel figure set.}
Expanding the controlled cross-lingual set beyond 13 figures would enable statistically robust per-chart-type cross-lingual analysis and provide a stronger basis for isolating language effects from content effects.

\paragraph{Dynamic evaluation with retrieval.}
The current evaluation is static: models receive a figure and prompt.
Future work could evaluate retrieval-augmented generation, where models access the source paper alongside the figure, testing whether additional context improves accuracy or introduces further bias.

\paragraph{Temporal model tracking.}
As VLMs are updated frequently, longitudinal evaluation of the same figures across model versions would reveal whether numerical precision and behavioural reliability improve with successive releases.

\paragraph{User studies.}
Our evaluation measures description quality from a technical perspective.
Complementary user studies with visually impaired researchers would reveal whether MQM-optimised descriptions actually serve accessibility needs, or whether the verbosity penalty disadvantages the models users prefer.

\section{Summary of Contributions}\label{sec:contributions}
%% ===================================================================

This thesis makes five contributions to the evaluation of vision-language models on scientific figures.

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{The SciFig benchmark.}
  A multilingual evaluation corpus of 1\,005 scientific figures across four typologically diverse languages (English, Bulgarian, Chinese, German), with 2\,252 atomic fact checklists and 630 capability questions spanning five reasoning categories.

\item \textbf{Atomic MQM.}
  A normalised scoring framework that adapts Multidimensional Quality Metrics to figure description, with atom-level accuracy and completeness tracking, severity-capped error weighting, and dual-judge evaluation.
  Human validation confirms strong agreement (ICC\,=\,.91, system-level $\rho\geq.95$).

\item \textbf{The A-R-I behavioural framework.}
  A three-axis model for characterising VLM behaviour under stress: Admittance (epistemic honesty), Resistance (adversarial robustness), and Inductance (contextual reasoning).
  The framework distinguishes faithful readers from silent fabricators and reveals behavioural profiles invisible to standard accuracy metrics.

\item \textbf{A 13-model empirical evaluation.}
  The largest comparative evaluation of VLMs on scientific figure understanding to date, spanning proprietary and open-source models from 4B to 235B+ parameters, with 26\,364 errors analysed across 2\,966 evaluations.

\item \textbf{Cross-cutting findings.}
  Numerical precision is the dominant bottleneck (30.6\% of errors).
  Description quality, comprehension, and behavioural reliability are partially dissociated capabilities.
  Robustness to image degradation is independent of clean-condition accuracy.
  Per-language comparisons require controlled parallel designs.
  Gemini 3.1 Pro is the only model that maintains top-tier performance across all four evaluation dimensions.
\end{enumerate}

\bigskip
\noindent
Returning to the four research questions posed in Chapter~\ref{chap:introduction}:
\textbf{RQ1} finds that GPT-5.2 leads description quality (75.5 MQM) but the top four models cluster within 5 points; German's apparent advantage is a dataset artefact.
\textbf{RQ2} shows that rotation and low contrast cause the largest degradation, and robustness is a distinct capability from baseline accuracy.
\textbf{RQ3} reveals that comprehension and description engage different model capacities, with Gemini overtaking GPT-5.2 on question answering.
\textbf{RQ4} demonstrates that the A-R-I framework exposes behavioural patterns invisible to accuracy metrics: GPT-5.2's resistance gap, the Gemma family's silent fabrication, and Gemini's balanced reliability across all axes.

Together, these findings argue that evaluating VLMs on scientific figures requires moving beyond single-score benchmarks toward multi-dimensional assessment of accuracy, robustness, comprehension, and behavioural trustworthiness.
